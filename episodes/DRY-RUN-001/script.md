# Script — DRY-RUN-001 The Bellweather Umbrella Bell

Target duration: 3 minutes

Target word count: 420–460 words

Status: Approved for dry-run validation only

## Hook (0:00–0:15)

In the fictional town of Bellweather, opening an umbrella indoors did not bring bad luck. It brought a bell, an attendant, and—according to local legend—a fine.

Visual intent: An umbrella opens; the mascot reacts as a large bell rings.

## Setup

Bellweather's covered market opened in 1884. Its grand roof kept out the rain, but shoppers carried the rain inside on hundreds of dripping umbrellas. The polished stone floor became a patchwork of puddles. [F01, F02]

The roof had solved the obvious weather problem while creating a less obvious indoor one. A visitor could step out of a storm, shake open an umbrella beneath the roof, and leave a slick trail exactly where the next shopper expected firm ground. The danger was ordinary, repeatable, and easy to miss until someone slipped.

Visual intent: Establish the market, then show raindrops becoming puddles and a slipping hazard.

## Story / explanation beats

### Beat 1

The market staff found a simple solution. Whenever someone opened a wet umbrella indoors, a clerk rang a brass bell. An attendant heard the signal, hurried over with a wooden stand, and stored the umbrella until its owner left. [F03]

The clever part was not the bell itself. It was the shared meaning. In a noisy market, one clear sound told the attendant what had happened and what to bring. The shopper needed no long explanation, the clerk did not need to leave the counter, and the dripping umbrella quickly had a safe place to wait.

Visual intent: A left-to-right diagram connects umbrella, bell, attendant, and stand.

### Beat 2

Years later, absorbent mats appeared at every entrance. The puddles disappeared, and the warning was no longer necessary. But the bell remained. Clerks rang it for tradition, visitors expected to hear it, and a practical safety signal slowly became a town ritual. [F04]

Once a routine becomes familiar, people can preserve it for a new reason. Staff may repeat it because that is how the market has always welcomed rainy-day shoppers. Visitors may enjoy taking part in something that feels local. The same ring can move from instruction, to habit, to identity—even while its original job fades from memory.

Visual intent: Before-and-after timeline contrasts puddles with dry mats while the bell persists.

Some residents later claimed the town had fined anyone who opened an umbrella inside. No surviving rule in our fictional premise proves that detail, so it belongs to the legend, not the factual chain.

Visual intent: A fine notice appears with a question mark and moves into a box labeled “Legend.”

## Payoff

That is how customs can outlive their causes. People remember the action—ring the bell—even after they forget the wet floor that made it useful.

The important distinction is between a supported chain and a satisfying embellishment. The umbrella, puddle, bell, stand, and mat form the chain in our premise. The fine makes the tale sharper, but sharper does not mean better supported.

Visual intent: The original causal chain fades, leaving the bell and a crowd repeating the ritual.

## Close

The Bellweather story is invented for this template test, but the question is useful for real history: when a tradition looks strange, what forgotten problem was it trying to solve?
